**Re:** Submission of "A falsification framework for causal claims in polygenic epidemiology: the insomnia–Alzheimer's disease test case" (Research article)

Dear Dr Begum,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript "A falsification framework for causal claims in polygenic epidemiology: the insomnia–Alzheimer's disease test case" for consideration as a Research article in *Genome Medicine*.

The manuscript addresses a problem at the heart of genomic medicine: how should genetically supported drug targets be graded before clinical investment? MR is now routinely used to nominate targets as causally validated, but the prevailing analytical workflow has no procedure for falsification—it accumulates supporting evidence and calls the result confirmed. This asymmetry creates risk. We demonstrate that a widely replicated MR finding (insomnia→AD, β = +0.10, P = 9.3 × 10⁻⁴; 2,462 instruments) collapses entirely after LD-clumping (P = 0.52–0.64) and sample-overlap correction (P = 0.43): the original signal was never wrong within its pipeline, but the pipeline had no way to falsify its own output. The same asymmetry has real translational consequences: HDL→CAD—a genetically "positive" association that torcetrapib clinical trials later disproved—would have been flagged as Grade C (shared liability rather than directed causation) by the framework, on genetic evidence alone, without waiting for a failed trial. Conversely, the LDL→CAD target (Grade A, all six gates passed with F = 182) and the protective education→AD signal (Grade A, with triglyceride mediation contributing ~9% of the total effect and cross-ancestry consistency) survive every gate the framework applies. The translational message is that genomic medicine needs a triage step between GWAS signal and target investment—and the framework provides one.

The six-level falsification framework operates across six independently diagnostic gates: association, robustness (LD-clumping and sample-overlap correction), genetic architecture (genome-wide rg versus locus-specific sharing), direct effect (multivariable MR), biological mediation (two-step protein and metabolite MR), and directional external validation. Each gate can independently downgrade a causal claim, and the cumulative output is a graded verdict from A (10–12/12; high confidence) to D (0–3/12; not supported). The framework was calibrated on twelve trait pairs spanning four disease domains (Lipid, Neuro, Metabolic, Inflammatory), assigning Grade A to four confirmed causal relationships (LDL→CAD, education→AD, BMI→T2D, height→CAD), Grade B to one borderline-protective signal (education→T2D, with mild pleiotropy), Grade C to four pleiotropy-driven or reclassified signals (including insomnia→AD and HDL→CAD), and Grade D to three cleanly null claims (BMI→AD, CRP→CAD, T2D→AD). A blinded inter-rater reliability analysis confirmed that the scoring rubric produces reproducible grades (grade-level Cohen's κ = 0.76, substantial; quadratic-weighted κ = 0.94, almost perfect; per-level κ = 0.89 across 72 scored items). A 43,200-replicate simulation with known ground truth confirmed operating characteristics of sensitivity 0.97 and specificity 1.00 in the ideal scenario, with FDR ≤ 0.18 under directional pleiotropy.

The study includes five East Asian cross-ancestry checks, directly addressing the multi-ancestry scope that *Genome Medicine* has consistently championed. The protective education→T2D signal replicated significantly in BioBank Japan (IVW β = −0.145, P = 0.012), the T2D→AD result remained null across ancestries (EUR vs GARD EAS: β = +0.006, P = 0.93), and a trans-ethnic insomnia→AD check (40 EUR LD-independent instruments transferred into the GARD East Asian AD cohort with 2,919 cases) returned a negative, non-significant estimate (β = −0.84, P = 0.28), directionally consistent with the framework-identified null. No framework-assigned direction was contradicted in any of the five checks. The EAS analyses were constrained by the currently available exposure GWAS landscape—the largest EAS insomnia case–control study (n = 389) is insufficient for instrument construction—but the concordant directional evidence across all feasible checks, combined with the simulation-based calibration, supports the framework's generalisability beyond European populations.

We believe *Genome Medicine* is the right venue for this work for three specific reasons. First, the journal occupies a unique editorial space at the intersection of genomic discovery and clinical translation: the question our framework answers—"should this genetically supported target be acted on?"—is precisely the translational decision the journal's readership faces daily, and the framework's drug-target case studies (HDL→CAD as a cautionary Grade C, LDL→CAD as a textbook Grade A, education→AD with a triglyceride-mediated protective component) are worked examples of that decision logic. Second, the journal's emphasis on multi-ancestry genomic evidence is directly served by the five cross-ancestry checks—including the trans-ethnic transfer of a null insomnia→AD signal—which transform the framework from a European-only proof-of-concept into a cross-population validation. Third, the framework's methodology (six-gate falsification, graded evidence thresholds, blinded inter-rater validation, simulation calibration) aligns with *Genome Medicine*'s publication record of methods-oriented papers that advance how genomic evidence is interpreted for clinical decisions.

We are transparent about what the framework cannot do. It cannot prove a causal effect; it can only raise or lower confidence in one. Its practical value is the default we hope the field will adopt: every MR-based report should present both clumped and unclumped IVW estimates as a routine sensitivity analysis, much as meta-analyses now report both fixed- and random-effect models. For reviewers and editors, the A/B/C/D rubric offers a transparent standard for judging whether a signal reflects directed causality or shared liability—a standard that the STROBE-MR reporting guideline defines but does not supply.
